01 Start here

What the MYP is

The Middle Years Programme (MYP) is a curriculum framework from the International Baccalaureate (IB) for students aged 11–16. The IB adopted it in 1994 as the second of its four programmes.

The most important idea to grasp first: the MYP is not a syllabus that tells you exactly what content to teach. In most subjects it deliberately does not prescribe specific content. Instead, it gives you a structure — concepts, contexts, skills, and assessment principles — that you wrap around your own national, state, or school curriculum.

★ Big Idea

The MYP is a framework for how students learn, not a fixed list of what they must memorize. Your existing subject content lives inside the framework.

The shape of the programme

  • Ages 11–16, designed as a five-year programme (often called MYP 1–5). Where five years isn't possible, schools may run a shorter route with IB authorization.
  • Eight subject groups, with at least 50 teaching hours per subject group, per year.
  • Offered only by authorized IB World Schools.
  • Sits in the middle of the IB continuum: PYP → MYP → DP / CP. It prepares students for the Diploma Programme (DP) and Career-related Programme (CP), but is also valuable on its own.

The eight subject groups

Language & Literature

The student's strongest language(s).

Language Acquisition

Individuals & Societies

Sciences

Biology, chemistry, physics, integrated.

Mathematics

→ Teacher Translation

Whatever subject you teach, you already belong to one of these eight groups. The MYP doesn't replace your subject — it gives you a shared language and a shared way of planning that connects your subject to the other seven.

02 The why

The purpose and philosophy of the MYP

Everything in the MYP flows from the IB mission: developing inquiring, knowledgeable, and caring young people who help build a better, more peaceful world through intercultural understanding and respect.

For early adolescents specifically, the MYP aims to develop students who are creative, critical, and reflective thinkers. It emphasizes intellectual challenge and encourages students to make connections between their studies and the real world — the years when young people are forming their identity and need learning that feels relevant and meaningful.

The philosophy in plain terms

Holistic

The whole learner

Intellectual, personal, emotional, and social growth — not just academic results.

International-mindedness

A wider view

Understanding that other people, with their differences, can also be right.

Conceptual

Deep over shallow

Learning organized around big transferable ideas, not isolated facts.

Connected

Real-world relevance

Learning placed in authentic contexts and linked across subjects.

★ Big Idea

The MYP isn't asking "What facts should students know?" first. It asks "What kind of thinker and person are we helping this young learner become?" — and designs learning backward from there.

03 Who it's for

The learner at the center

In the MYP, the student — not the teacher and not the textbook — sits at the centre of the design. The whole framework is arranged around the learner.

A useful way to picture this is as a set of nested layers. The learner is in the middle. Around them sit the tools the MYP uses to make learning meaningful: concepts, global contexts, and approaches to learning. All of this is held together by inquiry and aimed at the IB Learner Profile.

→ Teacher Translation

Your role shifts from "deliverer of content" toward "designer and facilitator of inquiry." You still teach — explicitly and often — but the goal is to set up experiences where students actively build understanding, with you guiding, modelling, and giving feedback.

Because the learner is central, inclusion and differentiation matter. The MYP expects teaching to be accessible to a wide range of learners, and it values knowing where each student is in their development — socially and emotionally as well as academically.

04 Ownership

Agency, voice, choice, and ownership

"Agency" is one of the most important words in current IB language. It means students having genuine influence over their own learning — and seeing themselves as capable of acting on the world.

Agency is usually described through three linked ideas:

Voice

Students' ideas, questions, and perspectives are heard and taken seriously in the learning.

Choice

Students make meaningful decisions — about topics, methods, products, or how they show learning.

Ownership

Students take responsibility for their progress, reflect honestly, and set their own goals.

★ Big Idea

Agency isn't "let students do whatever they want." It's the deliberate, scaffolded handing-over of decisions and responsibility so students grow into self-directed, reflective learners.

▶ Classroom Example

In a science unit on energy, instead of assigning one fixed lab, you let students choose which renewable source to investigate and how to present their findings (report, model, infographic, or short film), while you hold the concept and the assessment criteria constant. Same learning target, real choice.

Self-directed learning is also a strong theme in the MYP's upcoming enhancement — see Section 19.

05 The aim

The IB Learner Profile

The Learner Profile is the IB's shared description of the kind of person every IB programme tries to develop. It is the same across the PYP, MYP, DP, and CP — the common "north star."

There are ten attributes. They are not graded, but they are the purpose behind the academics. You'll see schools name and celebrate them constantly.

Inquirers

Curious; love learning.

Knowledgeable

Explore ideas of significance.

Thinkers

Reason critically and creatively.

Communicators

Express themselves confidently.

Principled

Act with integrity and fairness.

Open-minded

Value other perspectives.

Caring

Show empathy and compassion.

Risk-takers

Approach challenge with courage.

Balanced

Care for mind, body, and others.

Reflective

Consider their own learning.

→ Teacher Translation

You don't need a separate "Learner Profile lesson." Instead, notice and name these attributes as they show up naturally: "That was real risk-taking, trying a method you weren't sure about." Over time this language becomes part of your classroom culture.

06 How students learn

Inquiry-based learning

Inquiry is the MYP's central way of learning. Rather than starting with answers to memorize, learning starts with questions worth exploring.

Inquiry doesn't mean "no teaching" or pure discovery. It means structuring units so students investigate, make sense of evidence, and build understanding — with plenty of guidance, modelling, and direct instruction along the way.

The engine of an MYP unit: the statement of inquiry

Each unit is anchored by a statement of inquiry — a single sentence that fuses a concept with a real-world context to express a transferable, "so-what" understanding.

Example statement of inquiry: "The stories communities tell shape how individuals understand their own identity."

Three kinds of inquiry question

Teachers usually plan questions at three levels to drive the unit:

Factual

What / when / how?

Knowledge and content. "What techniques did the artist use?"

Conceptual

Why / how connected?

Transferable ideas. "How does form communicate meaning?"

Debatable

To what extent?

No single answer. "Is some art more 'valid' than others?"

▶ Classroom Example

You open a maths unit not with "Today: the Pythagorean theorem," but with: "How do builders make sure a corner is truly square?" Students investigate, hit the need for the theorem, and learn it as a tool that answers a real question.

07 Connecting subjects

Interdisciplinary learning

! A quick terminology note

You may hear the word "transdisciplinary." That term belongs to the IB's Primary Years Programme (PYP). In the MYP, the equivalent connected-learning approach is called interdisciplinary learning. They are related ideas but not the same thing — using the right word matters in IB conversations.

Interdisciplinary learning means combining the knowledge and skills of two or more subject groups to create a new, integrated understanding that neither subject could reach alone.

It is a formal MYP requirement: students engage in at least one collaboratively planned interdisciplinary unit in each year of the programme. There are even dedicated interdisciplinary assessment criteria, and (for schools doing the IB's external assessment) an on-screen Interdisciplinary Learning examination.

Three ways subjects can relate — and where each belongs.
ApproachWhat it meansWhere it lives
DisciplinaryLearning within one subject's tools and ways of thinking.Everyday MYP teaching
InterdisciplinaryTwo+ subjects integrated to build understanding neither reaches alone.MYP (≥1 unit per year)
TransdisciplinaryLearning organized around real-world themes that cut across all subjects.PYP (primary years)
▶ Classroom Example

Science and Individuals & Societies co-plan a unit on water scarcity. Students use scientific data on the water cycle and geographic analysis of population and policy to propose realistic solutions — reaching an understanding that pure science or pure geography couldn't give them.

08 Real-world lenses

The six Global Contexts

! Terminology note

The "six transdisciplinary themes" are a feature of the PYP. The MYP's equivalent is the six Global Contexts. The IB describes them as developed from, and extending, the PYP's transdisciplinary themes — so they're cousins, but the MYP uses the global-context language.

Global contexts answer the question every adolescent silently asks: "Why are we learning this?" They place subject content inside an authentic, real-world setting. Each MYP unit selects one global context to focus the inquiry.

Identities & relationships

Who am I? Who are we? Belonging, health, relationships, what it means to be human.

Orientation in space & time

Where and when? Journeys, turning points, histories, our place in the cosmos.

Personal & cultural expression

How do we express ourselves? Creativity, beauty, culture, meaning.

Scientific & technical innovation

How does the world work? Discovery, invention, and its impact on people and environments.

Globalization & sustainability

How is everything connected? Systems, interdependence, and shared decisions.

Fairness & development

What are the consequences of our choices? Rights, justice, access, and equity.

→ Teacher Translation

Pick the context that gives your unit genuine real-world purpose, then keep coming back to it — not just as a hook on day one. The context should shape your inquiry questions and the way students apply what they learn. Over the programme, students should experience all six.

09 Big transferable ideas

Concepts and conceptual understanding

Concepts are the MYP's "big ideas" — powerful, transferable mental tools that organize learning and let understanding travel from one topic (or subject) to another.

The MYP uses two layers of concepts:

Key concepts (16)

Broad, interdisciplinary ideas shared across the whole programme. Each unit usually centres on one.

Related concepts

More specific ideas tied to a particular subject group, which sharpen and deepen the key concept within that discipline.

★ Big Idea

Facts are the "stuff" of a lesson; concepts are what the facts are for. A student who understands "systems" in biology can transfer that thinking to economics, ecosystems, or government — that transfer is the whole point.

◆ Upcoming change

Under the MYP's planned enhancement, "key" and "related" concepts are proposed to be combined into a single list called "specified concepts" for each subject group, with schools still able to add their own. 10 Learning how to learn

Approaches to Learning (ATL)

Approaches to Learning are the skills that help students "learn how to learn." They run through every subject and are the connective tissue of the whole programme.

The IB groups ATL into five skill categories, which are broken down further into more specific skill clusters:

Communication

Exchanging information through interaction and literacy.

Social

Collaboration — working well with others.

Self-management

Organization, affective (managing state & focus), and reflection.

Research

Information literacy and media literacy.

Thinking

Critical thinking, creative thinking, and transfer.

→ Teacher Translation

Don't try to teach all five everywhere. For each unit, choose a small number of ATL skills that the work genuinely demands, then teach them explicitly — model the skill, give students chances to practise, and have them reflect. ATL skills aren't graded as a separate subject, but they shape performance in every subject and in the projects.

▶ Classroom Example

Before a group research task, you spend ten minutes explicitly teaching one research skill (evaluating source reliability) and one social skill (assigning and rotating roles). You name them, model them, and ask students to reflect on them afterward.

11 Learning that does something

Action in the MYP

The MYP expects learning to lead somewhere. Its form of meaningful action is called Service as Action — students applying what they learn to make a positive difference.

Action grows naturally out of inquiry: when students understand something real, they're moved to respond to it. Service as Action is woven through the programme and is formalized in the MYP Projects (see Section 13).

Action takes many forms

It does not have to be a big charity event. The IB recognizes a wide range of legitimate responses, including direct service, indirect service, advocacy, and research — and increasingly, relationship-building, dialogue, and reflection.

★ Big Idea

The aim isn't "doing good deeds for points." It's helping students see themselves as people whose learning gives them the capacity — and responsibility — to act thoughtfully in their communities.

◆ Upcoming change

The MYP enhancement proposes making a link to community engagement an expectation in every unit, with broader recognition that engagement can happen through dialogue, relationships, and reflection — not only direct action. In development

12 How learning is judged

Assessment in the MYP

MYP assessment is criterion-related: you judge a student's work against published descriptors of quality — not against other students, and not by adjusting to the class average.

How it works

  • Each subject group has four assessment criteria (A, B, C, D). The names differ by subject (e.g. "Knowing and understanding," "Communicating").
  • Each criterion is marked on a 0–8 scale against fixed level descriptors, in bands (1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8).
  • You apply the descriptors using "best-fit" professional judgement — the descriptor that, overall, best matches the work.
  • Final reporting grades come from looking across several tasks, not averaging one number.
Criterion A6/8
Criterion B7/8
Criterion C5/8
Criterion D6/8

Four criteria, each out of 8 → a total out of 32, converted to a final 1–7 grade using IB boundaries.

! The single most common mistake

The four criterion scores are not percentages, and you do not simply average them to get the grade. A "6" describes a level of quality. The total out of 32 is converted to a 1–7 grade using IB grade boundaries, informed by your best-fit judgement across the body of work.

Fairness across schools

The IB keeps a "7" meaning the same everywhere through standardization (teachers within a school aligning their judgements) and moderation (the IB checking samples). There are also shared command terms (like analyse, evaluate, justify) that define exactly what a task is asking for.

External validation: eAssessment

Schools may choose the IB's external eAssessment in the final year, which is the route to an IB-validated MYP Certificate. It combines on-screen examinations (in subjects such as Language & Literature, Individuals & Societies, Sciences, Mathematics, and Interdisciplinary Learning) with ePortfolios (for areas such as Language Acquisition, Arts, Design, and Physical & Health Education). Schools that don't take eAssessment may instead award a school-based certificate or a Record of Participation, which are not IB-validated.

◆ Upcoming change

The IB is continuing a gradual shift toward more on-screen assessment, with more components expected to move on-screen over time. Exact subject lists and dates are to be confirmed by official IB guidance.

13 The signature projects

The MYP Projects

! Terminology note

The "Exhibition" is the culminating experience of the PYP. The MYP's culminating, student-directed work is the Personal Project (with the Community Project earlier in the programme). These are the MYP's equivalent signature projects.

The MYP Projects are where agency, ATL skills, inquiry, and action all come together in one extended, independent piece of work that students largely drive themselves.

Final year

The Personal Project

Required of every student in the final year (MYP 5). A sustained, self-directed exploration: the student sets a goal, creates a product or outcome, keeps a process journal, and writes a report reflecting on the journey.

Assessed against published criteria — currently Planning, Applying skills, and Reflecting — and externally moderated by the IB.

Years 3 or 4

The Community Project

For schools whose programme ends before MYP 5 (or as an earlier milestone), often done in small groups. It focuses on service and a genuine community need.

Assessed against four criteria: Investigating, Planning, Taking action, Reflecting.

→ Teacher Translation

Even if you never supervise a project, your everyday teaching prepares students for it. Every time you make ATL skills explicit — goal-setting, research, time management, reflection — you're building the exact capacities the Personal Project will demand.

14 Working together

Planning and collaboration

The MYP is a collaborative programme. Teachers plan together, share a common language, and articulate learning vertically (year to year) and horizontally (across subjects).

The MYP unit planner

Units are documented on a shared unit planner. It's built through "backward design" — you decide the understanding and assessment first, then design the learning. A planner typically pulls together:

Key concept(s)

The big transferable idea.

Related concept(s)

The subject-specific lens.

Global context

The real-world setting.

Statement of inquiry

The unit's core understanding.

Inquiry questions

Factual, conceptual, debatable.

ATL skills

The skills you'll teach explicitly.

Content

Your subject/national curriculum.

Assessment

Tasks & criteria, plus reflection.

★ Big Idea

The unit planner isn't paperwork for its own sake — it's the conversation. It forces a team to agree on why a unit matters and how it connects, before worrying about activities.

A MYP coordinator supports this work: helping teams plan, ensuring the framework is applied consistently, and connecting the school to official IB requirements and updates. When you're unsure, the coordinator is your first stop.

15 The practical shift

What this means in the classroom

You don't need to reinvent everything overnight. Most of what you already do still applies — the MYP reframes it. Here are the day-to-day shifts.

Plan from a concept, not just a topic

Ask "what big idea is this really about?" before listing content.

Write a statement of inquiry

One sentence that captures the transferable understanding of the unit.

Choose a global context — and keep using it

Let it shape your questions and tasks, not just your opening hook.

Name a few ATL skills and teach them

Model them, practise them, reflect on them explicitly.

Use criteria and descriptors openly

Share them with students so they know what quality looks like.

Assess by best-fit, not by averaging

Match work to the descriptor that fits overall.

Build in voice and choice

Give students real, scaffolded decisions.

Connect to the real world & community

Make the "why does this matter?" visible.

→ Teacher Translation

If you only changed one thing this term, change how you start a unit: lead with a concept and an inquiry question instead of a list of content. Almost everything else follows from that one move.

17 Avoid these traps

Common misconceptions for new MYP teachers

These are the misunderstandings that trip up almost everyone at first. Spotting them early saves a lot of confusion.

Myth"The MYP tells me exactly what content to teach."
RealityIt's a framework. In most subjects you choose the content and embed your national/school curriculum inside it.
Myth"The MYP has transdisciplinary themes and an Exhibition."
RealityThose are PYP features. The MYP uses Global Contexts and interdisciplinary learning, and culminates in the Personal Project.
Myth"The criterion scores get averaged into the grade."
RealityYou use best-fit judgement against descriptors; the total out of 32 converts to a 1–7 grade via IB boundaries.
Myth"A score of 8 is like 100%."
Reality0–8 describes a level of quality against fixed descriptors — it is not a percentage.
Myth"ATL skills are a separate subject to grade."
RealityATL skills are taught explicitly across all subjects but aren't graded as their own subject — they support performance everywhere.
Myth"The global context is just a lesson starter."
RealityIt should run through the whole unit, shaping inquiry and application — not just appear on day one.
Myth"Every unit must be interdisciplinary."
RealityMost units are single-subject. The requirement is at least one interdisciplinary unit per year.
Myth"Service/action must be a big event."
RealityIt can be small and varied — including reflection, dialogue, and relationship-building, not only large projects.
Myth"Inquiry means I stop teaching directly."
RealityInquiry includes direct instruction, modelling, and clear guidance — it's structured, not hands-off.
Myth"eAssessment is compulsory."
RealityIt's optional — but it's the only route to an IB-validated MYP Certificate.

19 Looking ahead

MYP 2026 and upcoming changes

The MYP is being refreshed. This section separates what is in effect now from what is still in development — and flags where details are not yet final.

! Read this carefully

The big upcoming change ("Enhancing the MYP") is still being finalized as this guide is written. Anything marked In development or To be confirmed should be verified against official IB sources before you act on it. Dates in particular have been reported differently in different places.

In effect now Current

  • The MYP's current model — eight subject groups, 16 key concepts + related concepts, six global contexts, ATL, service as action, interdisciplinary learning, and criterion-related assessment — remains in place.
  • Current MYP regulations and assessment procedures (the "MYP 2026" documents) govern programmes today.
  • A number of subject guides have been refreshed for recent first-teaching cycles, with the usual one-year transition windows.
  • The gradual move toward more on-screen eAssessment continues.

Enhancing the MYP In development

The IB ran a beta trial in 2025 of a reviewed MYP, and its own beta materials describe the enhanced programme as scheduled for full release in 2027. The stated goals are to make the MYP easier for educators, more flexible for schools, and better for learners. Proposed elements include:

Clearer, condensed aims

The programme's aims reframed around foundational themes such as knowledge & transfer, engagement, skills & competencies, and self-directed learning.

"Specified concepts"

Key + related concepts proposed to merge into one list per subject group (schools may still add their own).

Community engagement in every unit

A community link expected in each unit, with broader forms of engagement recognized.

More school flexibility

Greater autonomy over scheduling and some assessment, especially in earlier years.

Better unit-planning guidance

Simpler, clearer programme and subject guidance for teachers.

A new digital platform

A move from static PDF guides toward an interactive online platform ("IB Exchange").

Reported sequencing To be confirmed

2025

Beta trial

Schools trialled enhanced elements and gave feedback to the IB.

2027

Full release / new guidance & platform

IB beta materials describe a 2027 full release; new guidance and the digital platform are associated with this point.

After release

First teaching, then first new-model eAssessment

Some practitioner summaries report first teaching and first eAssessment under the new model arriving in later years. These dates are not consistent across sources and must be confirmed by official IB guidance.

→ Teacher Translation

You don't need to learn a "new MYP" to start teaching well now — the current framework is what's live. As a new teacher, focus on today's model, and let your coordinator filter official enhancement updates to your team as they're confirmed. Watch for changes to concept language, community engagement expectations, and unit-planning guidance.
